Saddam van der Linde

Omar Haroon – 18/11/2026

(Spoilers for the Red Dead Redemption series)

My friends,

“We can’t fight nature… we can’t fight change”.

It is the eve of GTA VI as I write this, and quite recently I went back and immersed myself in Rockstar Games’ Red Dead Redemption series. I found myself more and more fascinated by Dutch van der Linde’s character, particularly the manner in which his leadership deteriorates as the story progresses. However, what interested me most was the rather peculiar resemblance I began noticing between Dutch and a man whose personality and political career I have spent a great deal of time reading about: Saddam Hussein.

I appreciate that comparing the former President of Iraq to a fictional American outlaw might initially appear somewhat ridiculous, given the obvious differences in their respective circumstances. Nevertheless, the more closely I examined their personalities, their relationships with those closest to them, and, above all, their reactions to worsening circumstances, the more compelling I found the comparison.

For those unfamiliar with the series, Dutch is the charismatic leader of a gang of outlaws navigating the final years of the American frontier. An educated and articulate man, he considers himself something of a revolutionary, believing that the gradual expansion of government authority and industrial capitalism represents an existential threat to the freedoms he and his companions have enjoyed for decades. Throughout the series, Dutch repeatedly assures his followers that he has a plan which, provided they remain loyal and have faith in him, will eventually secure their freedom.

I would argue that the similarities between Dutch and Saddam extend well beyond their shared charisma, authoritarian tendencies and remarkable ability to deliver a convincing speech. What makes the comparison so interesting is the manner in which both men gradually came to regard their personal authority as inseparable from the survival of the people they claimed to represent.

The Family and the Nation

One of the defining characteristics of Dutch’s leadership is his insistence that the Van der Linde gang represents something far greater than a collection of criminals. Having spent decades gathering people who had either been abandoned by society or fallen foul of the law, Dutch successfully established an organisation whose members genuinely regarded one another as family.

Moreover, he provided them with a political philosophy which, for all its contradictions, gave their criminal activities an apparent moral justification.

According to Dutch, the gang was not motivated by greed or personal enrichment, but rather by a desire to escape the increasingly oppressive institutions of American society. Their robberies were therefore portrayed as a necessary means to preserve their independence, whilst their ever more violent confrontations with the authorities were explained as an unavoidable consequence of their refusal to surrender their freedom.

I do not necessarily believe Dutch was entirely insincere in these convictions. Indeed, the accounts provided by Arthur Morgan and Hosea Matthews suggest that Dutch was once a far more principled man. However, as the gang’s circumstances deteriorated, his understanding of those principles became harder and harder to distinguish from his personal desire to remain in command.

This is where I began noticing similarities with Saddam.

Much of Saddam’s political identity was built around the idea that his leadership was essential to the preservation of Iraq’s sovereignty, dignity and position within the Arab world. Through the Ba’ath Party’s extensive propaganda apparatus, Saddam was presented not merely as the President of Iraq, but as the embodiment of the country’s historical aspirations and national identity.

A mural of Saddam in profile beside an ancient Mesopotamian king, set against the walls of an ancient city.

The Iran-Iraq War, for instance, was incorporated into a wider historical narrative through its association with the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah, the seventh-century Arab victory over the Sasanian Empire. Similarly, the confrontation with the American-led coalition following Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait was designated the Mother of All Battles, a title which continued to feature prominently in official Iraqi rhetoric despite the disastrous military outcome. Saddam’s relationship with Saladin is perhaps the clearest example of this. Both men were born in Tikrit, and Saddam rarely missed an opportunity to remind Iraqis of it, presenting himself through murals, posters and official rhetoric as the modern successor to the man who recaptured Jerusalem from the Crusaders in 1187. Much as Saladin had united the Muslim world against a foreign occupier, Saddam was presented as the leader destined to do the same, and the liberation of Jerusalem became a recurring promise of his presidency, culminating in the formation of the so-called Jerusalem Army in 2001, which, rather tellingly, never went anywhere near Jerusalem. The irony, of course, is that Saladin was a Kurd, a detail the regime’s propaganda was considerably less eager to emphasise, particularly given what Saddam’s government did to the Kurds of the north only a few years earlier.

A mural tying the Iran-Iraq War to the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah. The banner reads: “Men are men… from the Qadisiyyah of Sa’d to the Qadisiyyah of Saddam.” Sa’d ibn Abi Waqqas is on the left, Saddam on the right.

A mural of Saddam on a white horse, sword raised, with the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque behind him and crowds carrying his portrait below.

What I find interesting about this approach is the extent to which it made acknowledging failure almost impossible. Having constructed a political identity in which Saddam’s decisions were presented as expressions of Iraq’s historical destiny, admitting that those decisions were mistaken would necessarily undermine the legitimacy of the man who made them.

Dutch encounters a remarkably similar problem. As the gang’s situation deteriorates, he repeatedly promises his followers that one final robbery will provide the money they need to abandon their criminal activities and escape to Tahiti, where they will bore off their lives harvesting endless fields of mangoes. However, each unsuccessful operation merely produces the need for another, more dangerous scheme. Whenever Arthur or John Marston begin questioning the wisdom of his decisions, Dutch interprets their concerns as evidence of disloyalty rather than legitimate disagreement.

In both cases, the leader’s personal credibility becomes so closely tied to the success of the collective enterprise that acknowledging a mistake threatens the foundations of his authority. Consequently, both men became more and more committed to preserving the appearance of progress, even when their decisions were producing precisely the opposite result.

Blackwater and Kuwait

The most striking similarity between the two stories concerns the disastrous decisions which fundamentally altered their respective trajectories.

For Dutch, that decision was the attempted ferry robbery in Blackwater, an operation which takes place shortly before the events of Red Dead Redemption 2. Although the player never actually witnesses the robbery, its consequences dominate much of the narrative. Having been persuaded by Micah Bell to pursue what was supposedly an exceptionally lucrative opportunity, Dutch proceeded with the operation despite Hosea and Arthur already developing an alternative scheme.

The robbery ultimately ended in disaster, with several members of the gang killed, Dutch himself shooting an innocent woman, and the survivors being forced to abandon their money and flee into the mountains. More importantly, the gang never truly recovered. Throughout the remainder of the game, Dutch repeatedly attempted to compensate for the losses incurred at Blackwater, yet each subsequent operation exposed the gang to further danger and gradually eroded the loyalty of its remaining members.

I cannot help but draw a comparison with Saddam’s decision to invade Kuwait in August 1990.

Having emerged from eight years of war with Iran, Iraq found itself facing substantial economic difficulties, including enormous debts owed to neighbouring Gulf states, declining oil revenues and the huge financial burden of reconstructing the country. Kuwait, in particular, had become a source of growing frustration for Saddam, who accused its government of exceeding agreed oil production levels and extracting Iraqi oil through alleged slant drilling.

Although these disputes were undoubtedly serious, Saddam’s decision to resolve them through the military occupation of Kuwait remains one of the most consequential political miscalculations in modern Iraqi history.

A 1990 mural of Saddam beside a muslim leader presumably Saladin, with horsemen charging on one side and Iraqi tanks and artillery on the other.

Much like Dutch’s decision to pursue the Blackwater ferry robbery, Saddam appears to have regarded an exceptionally ambitious and dangerous operation as a means of escaping a worsening financial and political situation. Both men must have also underestimated the scale of the response their actions would provoke.

The consequences of Kuwait, however, extended far beyond the military defeat Iraq suffered in 1991. I would argue that the following thirteen years of Saddam’s presidency were largely defined by the political, economic and diplomatic consequences of that single decision. The uprisings which followed the war, their brutal suppression, the imposition of international sanctions, the weapons inspection regime and Iraq’s growing isolation all contributed to a situation from which Saddam was never able to extricate the country.

Similarly, Dutch spends the remainder of Red Dead Redemption 2 attempting to restore the position his gang enjoyed before Blackwater, despite the increasingly obvious reality that the circumstances which made their former lifestyle possible had fundamentally changed.

In both cases, two leaders, confronted with deteriorating circumstances, embarked upon exceptionally risky operations intended to reverse their fortunes, only to find themselves trapped by the consequences. Rather than reconsidering the assumptions behind their original decisions, both continued pursuing ever more ambitious strategies in the belief that one final success would somehow make up for everything that came before it.

The Yes-men

Another interesting aspect of the comparison concerns the people who surrounded both leaders, and the extent to which their influence shaped the decisions which ultimately brought about their downfall.

Within the Van der Linde gang, Hosea Matthews occupies a rather unusual position. Having known Dutch for more than twenty years, Hosea is not simply a senior member of the organisation, but one of its original founders and among the very few people capable of openly challenging Dutch’s judgement. Unlike Dutch, whose elaborate schemes frequently depend upon violence and confrontation, Hosea generally favours negotiation and carefully calculated operations which minimise unnecessary risks.

His death during the Saint Denis bank robbery therefore represents far more than the loss of an experienced associate. It removes one of the last remaining people capable of moderating Dutch’s increasingly reckless behaviour.

I find a similar dynamic in Saddam’s relationship with Adnan Khairallah, his cousin, brother-in-law and childhood companion, who served as Iraq’s Minister of Defence throughout the Iran-Iraq War. Adnan enjoyed considerable respect within the Iraqi military establishment and, owing to his longstanding personal relationship with Saddam, was reportedly given a degree of latitude to disagree which few other officials could have exercised.

However, relations between the two men appear to have deteriorated towards the end of the 1980s, amid tensions within Saddam’s family and the growing influence of his son-in-law, Hussein Kamel.

In May 1989, Adnan died when his helicopter crashed during a sandstorm. Although the official explanation attributed the crash to adverse weather conditions, suspicions surrounding his death have persisted for decades. I have never encountered conclusive evidence that Saddam had anything to do with it, but come on.

Irrespective of the circumstances surrounding his death, Adnan’s absence from the Iraqi leadership during the months preceding the invasion of Kuwait raises an interesting question.

Would Saddam have reached the same decision had Adnan remained Minister of Defence?

I am not suggesting that Adnan would necessarily have prevented the invasion. However, considering their relationship, I do wonder whether he might have been better positioned than most to challenge the assumptions underlying such an extraordinary decision.

The same question can be asked of Hosea, whose continued presence may well have moderated some of the decisions which ultimately destroyed the gang.

What makes this comparison even more interesting, however, is what happened to Dutch following Hosea’s death.

As Arthur and John began questioning his judgement, Dutch placed greater and greater trust in Micah Bell, a comparatively recent addition to the gang whose principal talent appeared to be telling Dutch precisely what he wanted to hear.

Micah gradually convinced Dutch that his oldest and most loyal companions could no longer be trusted, whilst he himself remained entirely devoted to the cause. The extraordinary irony, of course, is that Micah ultimately proved to be an informant, having helped destroy the very organisation whose survival he supposedly valued above everything else.

Although the circumstances were very different, I find certain aspects of Micah’s relationship with Dutch reminiscent of Hussein Kamel’s rise within Saddam’s inner circle.

Having married Saddam’s daughter Raghad in 1983, Hussein Kamel rapidly accumulated substantial influence within Iraq’s military and security establishment, eventually acquiring responsibilities extending to the Republican Guard and the country’s military-industrial programmes. His rise was closely associated with his personal relationship with Saddam, and his growing influence reportedly generated a great deal of resentment among other senior officials.

Yet, in August 1995, Hussein Kamel defected to Jordan and publicly called for the overthrow of the very government which had afforded him such extraordinary privileges. Having subsequently returned to Iraq in February 1996 following assurances that he would be pardoned, he and his brother were killed within days.

Although Hussein Kamel and Micah were fundamentally different figures, it is rather amusing that both ultimately betrayed the leaders whose confidence they had spent years cultivating, only to meet their deaths in circumstances involving those very same men.

More importantly, both relationships illustrate the dangers of confusing loyalty with obedience.

The irony in both cases is that the people who appeared most willing to indulge the leader’s convictions ultimately proved capable of betraying him, whereas those who challenged his decisions were frequently among his most genuinely loyal associates.

Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum makes a similar observation about Saddam in his memoirs, noting that he had become accustomed to hearing what he wanted to hear.

When considered alongside Dutch’s growing reliance upon Micah, the resemblance becomes difficult to ignore. Both men surrounded themselves with people capable of recognising the weaknesses in their decisions. However, the environments they cultivated rewarded those who concealed such weaknesses rather than those who attempted to address them.

I’m Not Fucking Leaving!!!

Perhaps my favourite part of the comparison concerns Dutch’s inability to contemplate a future in which the gang continues without him.

Throughout RDR2, various opportunities arise for individual members to escape the increasingly dangerous circumstances in which they find themselves. However, Dutch persistently insists that the gang must remain together, maintaining that their collective survival depends upon their continued loyalty to his leadership.

I have often wondered whether Dutch genuinely believed his followers were incapable of surviving without him, or whether he simply could not imagine an existence in which he was no longer surrounded by people who depended upon him. Personally, I suspect both explanations contain an element of truth.

A particularly striking parallel emerges when examining the diplomatic efforts to persuade Saddam to relinquish power shortly before the American invasion of Iraq in March 2003. Among those who tried to convince Saddam to step aside was Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum, who recounts in his memoirs a meeting lasting more than five hours during which he attempted to persuade Saddam that his departure could spare Iraq the devastating consequences of another war.

Sheikh Mohammed even offered Saddam refuge in Dubai, arguing that his continued presence in power was placing the country at serious risk. Saddam, however, refused, responding that he was concerned with saving Iraq rather than saving himself.

I can genuinely imagine Dutch responding in precisely the same manner.

From Saddam’s perspective, relinquishing power may genuinely have appeared tantamount to abandoning Iraq at the moment of its greatest vulnerability. Given the subsequent collapse of Iraqi state institutions and the years of violence, insurgency and chaos which followed the invasion, his concerns regarding the consequences of regime change cannot simply be dismissed as entirely unfounded.

However, there is an obvious contradiction in the proposition that the only man capable of preserving a nation is the very leader whose decisions have placed it in such a precarious position.

Dutch’s reasoning follows a remarkably similar pattern. As the Van der Linde gang approaches its inevitable collapse, he continues insisting that its survival depends upon remaining united under his leadership, despite the growing evidence that his own decisions are accelerating its destruction.

Whenever his followers suggest that separation may offer them a better chance of survival, he interprets their proposals as an abandonment of the family. For both men, their understanding of leadership appears to have left little room for the possibility that stepping aside might itself be an act of loyalty towards the people they claimed to protect. And in both cases, the leader’s refusal to contemplate his own departure contributed to the destruction of the very organisation (or nation) whose survival he regarded as paramount.

They Will Find Another Monster

Towards the end of the original Red Dead Redemption, Dutch finds himself cornered by John, having lost virtually everything that once gave his life purpose.

Before his death, however, Dutch warns John that once the authorities have eliminated him, they will simply find another monster to pursue, because the continued existence of such enemies provides a justification for the institutions responsible for hunting them.

This is one of the most interesting observations in the entire series, particularly because the rest of the game appears to vindicate it. Having compelled John to eliminate the surviving members of his former gang, the federal authorities eventually turn against John himself, despite his attempts to abandon his criminal past and establish a peaceful life with his family.

I find an interesting parallel in Saddam’s warnings concerning the consequences of an American occupation of Iraq.

Following his capture in December 2003, Saddam reportedly warned his American interrogators that governing Iraq would prove far more difficult than they anticipated. Subsequent events demonstrated that his assessment was not entirely misplaced.

Although the American-led coalition succeeded in removing Saddam’s government within a matter of weeks, establishing a stable political order proved substantially more complicated. The dissolution of the Iraqi army, the implementation of de-Ba’athification measures and the collapse of existing state institutions contributed to a vacuum in which insurgent organisations and sectarian militias acquired considerable influence.

Moreover, the emergence of ISIS, whose leadership included men formerly associated with Saddam’s military and security establishment, illustrated the extent to which the consequences of Iraq’s political collapse would continue shaping the region long after Saddam himself had disappeared.

And then, just as Dutch predicted, the hunt simply moved on to the next monster. Abu Musab al-Zarqawi was killed in 2006 and Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi in 2019. In January 2020, an American drone strike outside Baghdad airport killed Qasem Soleimani, the commander of Iran’s Quds Force, along with Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis, the deputy head of Iraq’s Popular Mobilisation Forces. The irony is that a number of these monsters only found their way into Iraq because Saddam was gone. For all his crimes, Saddam spent eight years at war with Iran and the following fifteen treating it as his greatest threat. He went as far as telling his FBI interrogator that his refusal to let weapons inspectors back into the country had more to do with fear of Iran discovering Iraq’s weaknesses than with any fear of the Americans.

Once Saddam was removed and the army dissolved, the wall he had built against Tehran came down with him, and Iran wasted little time filling the vacuum through the militias it armed, funded and trained. The same Americans who toppled Saddam then spent the next two decades hunting the new monsters that had grown out of the space he left behind, each one justifying the hunt for the next, exactly as Dutch said they would.

I think there is an important distinction between recognising the accuracy of a particular observation and endorsing the man responsible for making it.

Saddam’s warnings about the difficulties of governing Iraq did not necessarily validate his own political decisions, just as Dutch’s criticism of the federal authorities did not absolve him of responsibility for the collapse of his gang.

Yet still both men demonstrated a remarkable ability to identify the weaknesses and contradictions of their adversaries, even whilst remaining unwilling to acknowledge comparable failings in themselves. This was one of the defining contradictions of their respective personalities. They were frequently perceptive when examining the conduct of others, yet stubbornly resistant to applying the same scrutiny to their own decisions.

Our Time Has Passed

The most significant difference between Dutch and Saddam emerges in the circumstances surrounding their deaths.

By the end of the original Red Dead Redemption, Dutch has finally come to recognise that the world in which he once flourished has disappeared. The expansion of federal authority, the growth of industrial capitalism and the gradual disappearance of the American frontier have rendered his former lifestyle impossible. When John finally corners him, Dutch acknowledges that their time has passed, before stepping backwards off a cliff rather than allowing himself to be captured.

Saddam, on the other hand, never seems to have accepted his removal from power. When American forces found him hiding near ad-Dawr in December 2003, he reportedly identified himself as the President of Iraq, months after his government had collapsed, and throughout his trial he repeatedly challenged the authority of the court and kept insisting that he was still Iraq’s legitimate President.

Even his execution in December 2006 reflected the extraordinary contradictions of his legacy. The widely circulated recording of his final moments, in which those present taunted him and chanted the name of Muqtada al-Sadr, transformed how a large part of Iraqi and Arab society saw him. For some, the footage only reinforced their condemnation of his government. For a lot of others, his composure at the gallows turned him into a symbol of defiance, at a time when the American occupation and Iraq’s sectarian divisions were both getting worse. In that sense, Saddam’s death arguably strengthened the very mythology he had spent his presidency cultivating.

Dutch, meanwhile, died having finally accepted that the world he spent his life trying to preserve was gone. Both men lost the authority they once exercised, but their endings reveal a real difference in how they confronted that loss. Dutch eventually accepted that his struggle had become impossible, whereas Saddam kept asserting his political identity until the very last moment of his life.

I Have a Plan

A tiled portrait of Saddam holding a rifle in front of the Dome of the Rock, on display at the Imperial War Museum, London. Photo by Matt Brown, Licensing here

Having considered all of this, I keep returning to the same question: what actually made Dutch and Saddam so compelling to the people who followed them? I do not believe it can be explained simply by fear, opportunism or manipulation. All three played a role, Saddam’s case most of all, but that explanation overlooks the genuine loyalty and admiration both men inspired among people who knew them personally. Arthur’s affection for Dutch was clearly sincere, even after his eventual disillusionment, and Saddam commanded genuine admiration among parts of Iraqi society whose support cannot be explained through coercion alone.

Both men possessed qualities which, under different circumstances, might have produced very different outcomes. They were charismatic, politically perceptive and capable of articulating grievances which resonated with their followers. Yet those same qualities became more and more destructive as their personal authority and cult of personality grew harder to challenge.

This may be why I find Dutch such a fascinating character, as an Iraqi who has grown up hearing very different accounts of Saddam’s presidency. I have always been interested in examining Saddam beyond the simplistic portrayals which tend to dominate discussions of his presidency. Not because I believe his political career requires rehabilitation, but because I find the contradictions in his personality, and the extraordinary loyalty he inspired among some of those closest to him, worthy of examination and observation.

In much the same way, I do not think Dutch can be properly understood by looking only at the reprehensible decisions he makes towards the end of Red Dead Redemption 2. He is also the man who raised Arthur and John, provided a home for people who had nowhere else to go, and inspired genuine affection among those who kept believing in him long after his judgement had begun to deteriorate. It is precisely this contradiction which makes his eventual downfall so compelling.

I would not suggest that Rockstar Games consciously modelled Dutch van der Linde upon Saddam Hussein, but I find it striking how closely the two narratives resemble one another when examined through the relationships, political convictions and personal insecurities of the men at their centre.

The most revealing similarity is that both spent much of their final years assuring those around them that they understood the situation better than anybody else, while the evidence that their decisions were producing disastrous consequences kept mounting. Whether it was Dutch promising that one more robbery would finally allow the gang to escape, or Saddam insisting that Iraq would ultimately prevail against ever more powerful adversaries, both appeared convinced that their leadership was essential to a victory which kept slipping further out of reach.

Saddam as an ancient Mesopotamian king, drawing a bow from a chariot over a fallen lion, with missiles, fighter jets and a helicopter overhead and a warship offshore.

I suppose the question I am left with is whether either man would have recognised himself in the other. That said, I doubt the two would have tolerated one another for very long. Both regarded themselves as indispensable, and I struggle to imagine either accepting a position in which somebody else was making the decisions, let alone responding well when informed that the other had a better plan.
